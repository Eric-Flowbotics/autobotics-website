Quote fast, follow up

How do you follow up on a quote without sounding pushy?

A Home Service Edge guide · by Eric Lenhardt

Guides Prompts

Send the same three short texts on a fixed schedule — the day after the quote goes out, again a few days later, and once more about a week on — written to sound like a busy professional checking in, not someone chasing money. Then, before you send them to a pile of quiet quotes, paste your list into a free AI assistant and have it rewrite each one to name the actual job, in your own words. That last step is what turns a template into twenty real messages, and it takes about five minutes. The reason any of it works is the part that stops most operators cold: following up on a quote isn't begging. It's service.

The full breakdown, on screen — or keep reading; everything in the video is written out below.

That's the answer. Here's why it's worth doing, and exactly how — with or without software.

Following up isn't begging — it's service

The reason you don't follow up isn't laziness. It's that following up feels desperate — like you're chasing somebody, or begging for a job you already bid. So you let it sit, and the quote dies of silence instead of a decision.

Think about what actually happened on the other end. The customer asked you for a price. You gave them one. Then life got in the way: your text got buried under forty other texts, they meant to reply, and they didn't. Nobody decided against you. The quote went cold on its own.

"Following up isn't begging. It's service." — Eric, in the video

And the quote you already sent is the cheapest job you'll ever chase. The drive out, the walkthrough, writing it all up — that money and time is already spent whether or not you ever hear back. On a few-hundred-dollar service call that stings; on a project bid it's a lot worse. Swap in whatever a normal job is worth to you.

"The follow-up is the cheapest part of the entire job. It's also the part almost nobody does." — Eric, in the video

You'll notice there's no percentage in this article for how much a follow-up wins back. That's deliberate. The numbers floating around the internet for this are unsourceable, and a fake-precise range would be its own small hype. The honest version is the mechanism: a quote nobody follows up on closes at whatever rate silence closes at, and a short, human check-in gives a busy customer the one easy chance to say yes.

The three texts that do the work

The texts are the whole fix — not the software. Same three messages wherever you send them; the only question is whether a CRM sends them for you or you send them yourself. Each one has a single job.

Day 1 — make sure it landed. You're not selling anything here. You're confirming the quote arrived and opening the door to questions.

"Hi [Name], it's [You] with [Company] — just making sure my quote for [the job] came through okay. Happy to walk through any of it. Any questions?"

Day 3 — give them a reason to answer today. A real spot on your calendar does this without any pressure. It's a door, not a push.

"Hi [Name], checking in on the [job] estimate. If you want to move forward, I can still get you on the schedule for [timeframe] — just say the word."

Day 7 — give them permission to say no. This is the one operators skip, and it works backwards from what you'd expect. What usually comes back is some version of "no, we still want it, sorry, things got busy." You gave them an easy out, they didn't take it, and that's your job back on the schedule.

"Hi [Name], I don't want to crowd your inbox — should I keep this quote open for you, or close it out for now? Either way is fine, just let me know where you landed."

No corporate language, no begging. Just a real person following up like a professional.

Make the three texts personal in about five minutes

Those three messages are a template — and a template sent to twenty people is still a blast. It's better than silence, but everybody can tell. So don't send the template. Use it as the tone sample, and have a free AI assistant write one real message per customer instead.

Here's the whole move, and it works on a phone:

1. Write out your quiet quotes from the last thirty days. Customer, what the job was, the price, how long it's been. A rough list in your notes app is fine.

"It doesn't need to be tidy. Mine never is." — Eric, in the video

2. Open a free AI assistant. ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini — any of them works, and the free version is enough. (In the video I run it in Claude, but nothing here is tool-specific.) Paste your list in with the prompt below, and paste in two or three of your own real texts where it asks, so it writes the way you write.

3. Read what comes back, fix anything that's off, and send.

What you get back is one message per customer, each one naming the actual job — the fence, the water heater, the move-out clean — instead of "your recent estimate." That specificity is the entire difference. The reason a follow-up feels like begging is that a generic blast feels like begging; a message that names the job you walked doesn't, because it isn't one.

There's exactly one rule attached to this, and it isn't optional. Read them before you send. You're the person who did the walkthrough — if something's off, you're the only one who'd catch it. The writing gets help; the sending stays human.

Twenty quotes takes about five minutes.

If you run a CRM, the follow-up can send itself

If you already pay for a field-service CRM, you don't need to buy anything — you need to switch on a feature that's almost certainly off and drop your three texts into it. All three of the big ones can automate quote follow-up. The catch that gets almost everybody: it's a paid tier or add-on you turn on, not something running by default.

Jobber builds this in as a quote follow-up automation, under the gear icon → Settings → Automations (or Settings → Emails and Text Messages if you don't see Automations). Per Jobber's own help doc, it sends up to two reminders on a day-based schedule you set, up to 90 days out, with message text you can customize, and each follow-up goes out in the same channel as the original quote. It's available on Jobber's Connect plan and up — Connect runs about $99/month billed annually. Load your day-1 and day-3 texts and it runs in the background.

Housecall Pro does it through Pipeline, which isn't part of the base plans — you add it to your account through a demo, and Housecall Pro doesn't publish a price, so confirm the cost when you ask. You'll find it under Pipeline → Automations → Estimates, and per Housecall Pro's help doc it gives you three estimate follow-ups — a first, second, and third — with the gap between each one set in days, so you can space them however you want. The email copy is editable; editing the text-message version needs Custom SMS switched on. One catch worth knowing before you rely on it: with Smart Recommendations enabled you can edit the wording but not the timing. The mechanics are the three-text ladder, automated.

ServiceTitan has it, with one distinction worth getting right. Its Follow Up screen is a manual worklist — its first tab is unsold estimates, and somebody in the office has to actually work it. The automated version lives in Autopilot, a library of prebuilt campaigns (some automated, some one-time) that's part of its Marketing Pro add-on. ServiceTitan itself says the platform is not optimized for a company with three or fewer technicians, so for most operators reading this it's worth naming, not buying.

The contrast is the whole point: the software only sends the texts on time. It doesn't write them, and it won't make them personal. If you're already paying for one of these, the follow-up you've been skipping is a setting, not a purchase — and don't leave the generic default text in there.

No CRM? Here's the honest version

You don't need a CRM for this, but you do need to know what your phone will and won't do.

"Your phone by itself will not follow up for you. It won't send three text messages on a schedule on its own. That's not a setting you're missing. It just doesn't do that." — Eric, in the video

There's no "this quote went cold" trigger sitting on your phone. So the no-CRM version is part reminder, part cheap tool — and either one works:

  • Three reminders you act on. Set one for day 1, day 3, and day 7 when the quote goes out, keep your three texts somewhere you can grab them fast, and send each in about twenty seconds when the reminder pops. Free, and it's the version most operators should start with.
  • Scheduled sends, if you're on Android. Google Messages will schedule a real text — type it, then touch-and-hold Send and pick the date and time. On iPhone, don't count on this: Apple's "Send Later" only works for blue-bubble iMessages, not regular texts to a customer's phone.
  • A cheap business texting app. Quo — the app formerly called OpenPhone — schedules your follow-ups, saves the three as reusable templates, and cancels a scheduled follow-up automatically if the customer already replied, so you never send an awkward check-in to someone who said yes yesterday.

Whichever one you pick, that's the entire setup question. The texts don't change.

The one thing to do Monday

Write out your quiet quotes from the last thirty days — customer, job, how long it's been. Paste that list into a free AI assistant with the prompt below. Read what comes back, fix anything that's off, and send them. Twenty quotes takes about five minutes.

Here's a list of quotes I've sent that haven't been answered. For each one, write me a short, friendly follow-up text that references the actual job — not a generic template. Warm and human, not pushy. Match the tone of these messages I've sent before: [paste two or three of your real texts]. Give me one message per customer, ready to send.

Every part of that prompt is doing a job. References the actual job is what stops it reading like a mail merge. Not pushy keeps it in service register instead of sales register. Match the tone of these messages is why it comes out in your words and not the internet's. One message per customer, ready to send is what makes it a five-minute task instead of a writing session.

"You're not starting over. You're picking up money you already left on the table." — Eric, in the video

What's next (the upgrade)

Once the pass is winning jobs back, hand the timing to something else — the CRM automation you already pay for, or a texting app that schedules for you — so day 1, day 3, and day 7 fire without you remembering. Same three texts, same five-minute personalizing pass on the ones that go quiet. Less remembering.

What you can skip: the expensive sales-automation suites. You'll see them promise to recover most of your dead leads for a few hundred dollars a month. Treat that as brochure math, not a number you'll see. You don't need a sales suite to send three text messages — you need the three texts, a reminder, and five minutes with a free AI assistant. Start with the cheap or free version; you can always add tools once the habit is making you money.

FAQ

How long should I wait before following up on a quote?

Send the first text the next day, while it's fresh — not weeks later. Then a second a few days on, and a third about a week out. The day-1/3/7 spacing is short enough to stay top of mind and slow enough that it never feels like nagging.

How many times should I follow up?

Three is plenty. The third text — "should I keep this open or close it out?" — is the natural stopping point, because it asks for a yes or a no instead of leaving the door open forever. If they go quiet after that, let it rest; you've made it easy to answer.

Can I use AI to write follow-up texts without them sounding like AI?

Yes, and the trick is what you feed it, not how you word the request. Paste in two or three real texts you've already sent, along with the actual job details for each customer, and ask for one message per customer rather than a template. The output sounds like you because you showed it what you sound like. Read every one before you send.

What if they tell me they went with someone else?

That's a win, not a loss. Silence teaches you nothing; a straight "we went another way" tells you where you stand and often why. Thank them, ask what made the difference if it feels natural, and you've learned something for the next quote.

Won't following up make me look desperate?

Not if the texts sound like the ones above. A short, friendly check-in that assumes the customer is just busy reads as a professional who's on top of their work — the opposite of desperate. The operators who look desperate are the ones who go silent and then panic-discount later.
